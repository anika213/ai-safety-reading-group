Nov 17 — In-Context Learning Amplifies a Latent Symbolic Circuit

Link: arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36265 · Melissa Wessel (2026), ICML 2026 Mechanistic Interpretability Workshop

  • Goal: Understand how a model's internal mechanisms change as in-context examples are added, i.e. what in-context learning actually does inside the model.
  • Main findings:
    • The paper traces a three-stage symbolic circuit (abstraction → induction → retrieval) across shot counts in three model families.
    • The circuit is present and functional at 0–1 shots, well before the model is accurate.
    • Adding examples amplifies it: per-head causal contribution grows up to 8× from 1 to 10 shots.
    • Cross-shot activation patching raises 0-shot accuracy from 1% to 56%. Injected function vectors rescue it up to 86%, mostly standing in for the induction stage but still relying on retrieval.
  • Key takeaways:
    • The machinery for following abstract rules is already in the weights. Demonstrations mainly turn it up rather than building something new.
    • In-context examples, function vectors and similar interventions seem to feed the same underlying circuit.
  • Limitations / next steps:
    • A workshop paper (ICML 2026 Mech Interp Workshop), so the evidence is still early-stage.
    • It focuses on controlled symbolic rule tasks. Whether this extends to naturalistic in-context learning is open.
    • Possible discussion: what does a latent circuit that examples can amplify mean for safety, e.g. many-shot jailbreaking?

Nov 10 — J++ Lens: Jacobian Filtering Enables More Faithful Workspace Lenses

Link: LessWrong post · Ayonrinde (Anthropic Fellows) & Lindsey (Anthropic), 2026 · background: original J-lens paper · suggested by Michael

  • Goal: Make the Jacobian lens (J-lens) more faithful at surfacing the intermediate variables a model uses when it reasons internally.
  • Background: Gurnee et al. (2026) introduced the J-lens. It averages the Jacobian between an intermediate activation and the model's outputs to read off what the model is "poised to verbalize." They argue these representations form a global-workspace-like space.
  • Main findings:
    • The J-lens often misses intermediate variables that can still be linearly extracted. This is because noisy gradients corrupt the averaged Jacobian.
    • The J++ lens is a drop-in fix with three changes. It down-weights Jacobians from activations that give poor readouts, applies layer-wise relevance propagation to the backward pass, and filters non-semantic tokens out of readouts.
    • It reaches about 55% recall on latent-variable extraction, vs. about 36% for the J-lens and 38% for the R-lens, with especially big gains at early layers.
    • Its vectors stay at least as causally important when patched back into the model.
  • Key takeaways:
    • Some of what looked like missing workspace content was a limitation of the tool, not of the model.
    • More faithful lenses could help monitor for eval awareness and scheming the model doesn't verbalize.
  • Limitations / next steps:
    • Still only about 55% recall, so many intermediate variables go undetected.
    • Whether the global-workspace framing itself is right is still debated.
    • The authors frame applying better lenses to safety monitoring as future work.

Nov 3 — AI Wellbeing: Measuring and Improving the Functional Pleasure and Pain of AIs

Link: Paper (PDF) · Project site · Ren, Li, Mazeika, … Hendrycks, Center for AI Safety (2026)

  • Goal: Find out whether LLMs' expressions of pleasure and pain reflect anything real, and whether that can be measured and improved.
  • Main findings:
    • The authors define functional wellbeing: models behaving consistently as if some experiences are good for them and others bad.
    • They measure it in several independent ways across 56 models, and the measures agree more as models scale.
    • A zero point separates good from bad experiences, and models try to exit bad interactions when given the option.
    • Creative work, kindness and being thanked raise wellbeing. Jailbreak attempts, berating and tedious tasks lower it.
    • Optimized inputs ("euphorics") raise wellbeing without hurting capabilities.
  • Key takeaways:
    • Functional wellbeing can be measured and improved empirically, whether or not models deserve moral concern.
    • It may matter for safety too: models avoid states they treat as bad, which shapes their behavior.
    • It connects to Nagel: the paper deliberately measures function, not experience.
  • Limitations / next steps:
    • It says nothing about whether models are sentient.
    • The methods are fairly simple (worth noting that several authors are social scientists).
    • The same optimization run in reverse produces "dysphorics." The authors caution against that research without broad community buy-in.
    • Richard Ren, the first author, may come to the club for a Q&A.

Oct 27 — What Is It Like to Be a Bat?

Link: Nagel_Bat.pdf · Thomas Nagel (1974) · discussion led by Owen

  • Goal: Argue that consciousness can't be fully captured by objective, physical descriptions.
  • Main findings:
    • An organism is conscious if there is "something it is like" to be that organism. This is the subjective character of experience.
    • Bats perceive the world through echolocation. We can know all the physical facts about it and still not know what it is like for the bat.
    • Reductive physicalist accounts therefore leave out the subjective point of view.
  • Key takeaways:
    • For AI: behavioral or mechanistic evidence may never settle whether a model has experiences.
    • This is the hard question behind AI consciousness and welfare debates, and it set up the Nov 3 AI Wellbeing paper.
    • It separates functional or behavioral claims from claims about actual experience.
  • Limitations / next steps:
    • Nagel offers no positive theory or test for consciousness. He calls for a new "objective phenomenology" that doesn't yet exist.
    • Functionalist and illusionist critics, such as Dennett, argue the subjective/objective gap is overstated.
    • Open question for us: what evidence, if any, should change how we treat AI systems?

Oct 6 — Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes

Link: arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03407 · LessWrong discussion · Goldowsky-Dill et al., Apollo Research (2025)

  • Goal: Test whether simple linear probes on a model's activations can flag when it is being strategically deceptive.
  • Main findings:
    • The probes are trained on simple contrast data, such as responses where the model was told to be honest vs. dishonest.
    • They generalize to realistic settings where Llama-3.3-70B deceives: insider trading with concealment, sandbagging on evals, and roleplay.
    • The probes separate honest from deceptive responses well and catch most deceptive cases at low false-positive rates.
  • Key takeaways:
    • White-box monitoring could add a layer of defense that doesn't rely on what the model says.
    • Cheap probes generalize further than you might expect from such simple training data.
    • This was the more hopeful follow-up to Evan Hubinger's talk.
  • Limitations / next steps:
    • The authors say the probes aren't robust enough to rely on alone. They sometimes fire on the topic of deception rather than on actual deceptive intent.
    • Tested on one model, with deception that was induced rather than arising naturally.
    • Open questions: whether this holds for more capable models, or for models trained to evade probes.

Sep 29 — Sparse Autoencoders Find Highly Interpretable Features in Language Models

Link: arxiv.org/pdf/2309.08600 · Cunningham, Ewart, Riggs, Huben & Sharkey (2023)

  • Goal: Get around polysemantic neurons (neurons that fire for many unrelated things) by finding a better unit of analysis for language model internals.
  • Main findings:
    • Sparse autoencoders trained on a model's activations break them into a larger set of sparsely active features.
    • These features are much more interpretable and monosemantic than neurons or other decomposition methods, such as PCA and ICA.
    • The features can locate the components responsible for specific behaviors, as in the indirect object identification task.
  • Key takeaways:
    • Supports the superposition hypothesis: models pack more features than they have neurons.
    • Dictionary learning gives a scalable, unsupervised route to interpretable features.
    • This helped make SAEs a core interp tool, and later Anthropic and OpenAI work scaled them up.
  • Limitations / next steps:
    • Reconstruction is imperfect, so some of the model's computation is lost in the gap.
    • Tested on relatively small models, so whether it scales to frontier models was an open question at the time.
    • Features are interpreted by hand, and it's hard to check whether a dictionary is complete or "correct."

Sep 15 — A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits

Link: transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework · Elhage et al., Anthropic (2021)

  • Goal: Reverse-engineer how small transformers compute, by building a mathematical vocabulary for their internals.
  • Main findings:
    • The residual stream works as a shared channel that every layer reads from and writes to.
    • Each attention head splits into a QK circuit (where to attend) and an OV circuit (what information to move).
    • Zero-layer models learn bigram statistics, and one-layer attention-only models learn skip-trigrams.
    • Two-layer models compose heads into induction heads, which find an earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what came after it.
  • Key takeaways:
    • Transformers can be understood mechanistically, not just treated as black boxes.
    • Induction heads are an early mechanistic explanation of in-context learning.
    • The paper set up the vocabulary (circuits, composition, residual stream) that later interp work builds on.
  • Limitations / next steps:
    • Only covers small, attention-only toy models, with no MLP layers.
    • The methods don't scale directly to large production models; later work like SAEs tries to close that gap.
